Most hobby irrigation systems threshold a raw sensor value and switch a pump.
That fails for a physical reason. Water takes time to percolate to the sensor,
so a reading taken immediately after watering reflects the soil before the water
arrived, and the controller keeps watering against a stale measurement. This
system was designed around the delay with a pulse-and-soak strategy: deliver a
short pulse, wait for percolation, re-read, and only then decide whether to
pulse again.

### Sensor selection as a characterization study

Both moisture-sensing candidates in the course kit were evaluated experimentally
and ruled out on measured grounds before a replacement was chosen.

A capacitive touch sensor was proposed to infer moisture from oscillation
period. Two measurable properties disqualified it. The first is timescale: soil
moisture moves over hours to days through evaporation and uptake, and the sensor
could not resolve gradual change between control updates even though it cleanly
separated fully saturated from fully dry on the bench. The second is dynamic
range: period variation between practical wet and dry conditions fell inside the
sensor's own noise floor, so repeat trials at the same true moisture returned
inconsistent readings.

A piezoelectric element was proposed to read soil compaction as a dryness proxy.
Testing showed it is a charge generator responding to dynamic force rather than
static load. Inserted and left stationary it produced a brief transient and
settled to zero regardless of moisture or compaction.

Both were replaced with a dedicated capacitive soil moisture probe. That was
chosen over the cheaper resistive style specifically because resistive probes
pass DC through the soil and corrode by electrolysis.

### Signal chain and calibration

The probe's analog output is read on a 12-bit converter and passed through a
Kalman filter with process and measurement noise tuned empirically, which holds
sample-to-sample variation under 1 percent volumetric water content in a stable
environment.

Calibration endpoints were measured directly: 1210 counts submerged and 2523
counts in open air, a 1313-count dynamic range. Those endpoints map onto an
approximate 10 to 50 percent volumetric water content scale for potting soil
rather than a naive 0 to 100 percent, because 100 percent volumetric water
content is physically impossible in soil. The endpoint values come from
published soil-science reference ranges.

Reservoir level is measured by an ultrasonic rangefinder mounted above the
reservoir, driven by a timer-based state machine with interrupt edge capture on
the echo line. Time of flight converts to distance through a least-squares fit
against measured known heights, is smoothed with a four-sample moving average,
and maps to a 0 to 100 percent level through a two-point field calibration.

### Control

The controller is a four-state machine updating every 500 ms: calibrate, idle,
water and soak. Idle reads moisture and reservoir level, and enters watering
only when the soil is below the trigger threshold and the reservoir is above its
own floor. Watering runs a single 500 ms pump pulse. Soaking waits 10 seconds
for percolation, re-reads, and either returns to idle on reaching target or
issues another pulse, with a hard cap of 10 pulses.

### Scope of the measurement

Volumetric water content here is approximate. True calibration requires
gravimetric oven-dry analysis, which was not performed. The endpoints are
specific to general potting soil and would move for a different medium, and
moisture is sampled at a single point rather than across the root zone. Those
bounds are stated because a moisture number without them is not a measurement.
