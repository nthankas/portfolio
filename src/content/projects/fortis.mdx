FORTIS is a tethered, holonomic inspection robot for the interior of General
Atomics' DIII-D tokamak. I was the technical lead on a five-person team and
owned the software stack, the simulation program, the perception and state
estimation, and the sizing of the chassis and arm. Teammates carried fabrication,
the power system and a share of the CAD under my direction.

The vessel sets the rules: a 15.75 inch entry profile, a 4.5 inch step to
straddle, a low-friction graphite floor, nothing may be touched, and no budget
for physical prototypes. Each section below is a problem that followed from
that and what I built to solve it. The full write-up linked above has the
figures, tables and demo clips for each.

### The physics engine cannot model an omni wheel

Isaac Sim has no anisotropic friction, so a wheel that grips along its drive
direction and rolls freely across it cannot be declared as a material. I turned
the property into geometry: each perimeter roller became a chain of five spheres
on a free axis, so the assembly rolls freely sideways while every sphere grips.
The known bias is about 20 percent extra torque on matched runs, which makes
every motor margin the simulation reported conservative by construction.

### The skid-steer chassis could not cross the step

I simulated the 90 degree point turn a skid-steer robot needs over the step
across 60 configurations. Three completed it, all at 85 to 116 degrees of tilt
and 7 to 10 inches of radial drift, because every wheel arc crosses the step
circle mid-turn. That study moved the robot to an X-drive on omni wheels, which
strafes the toroidal orbit with no yaw and never crosses the step at all.

### Sizing motors for a floor nobody could test on

One full orbit at each speed from 0.10 to 0.30 m/s, straddling the step, arm
stowed. Worst-wheel 95th-percentile torque reaches 4.10 Nm against 4.98 Nm of
continuous capability, with the conservative wheel model in place.

### Sizing arm actuators over a joint space that is mostly invalid

Most of the arm's joint space puts it through the chassis, the floor or the
vessel wall. I wrote a constraint-aware Monte Carlo: 5,000 poses with the 3 lb
payload in every sample, filtered to the 2,014 that are physically reachable,
each checked for tip-over at the lightest plausible chassis mass. The same
filtered set fed both the joint torque and the chassis stability analyses, so
the two results describe the same robot. Every joint peaks below 60 percent of
its continuous rating.

### Motion must never happen outside a permitted mission phase

A table-driven mission state machine with nine states and fifteen events
authorizes all motion. It has no middleware or hardware dependencies, so the
transition table was exercised exhaustively before it ran on a robot. The drive
and arm components sit above it and reject any command outside a permitted
phase, emitting an explicit stop on rejection. Any state can fault through an
unguarded wildcard, and leaving the fault state requires operator
acknowledgement, so recovery is never automatic.

<Figure id="fortis-mission-states" />

The X-drive kinematics constants are pinned to the robot description by a
regression test, so the model the simulation sized against and the code the
robot runs cannot drift apart quietly. Arm step timing lives on a dedicated
microcontroller, because a step pulse delayed by the host scheduler is a lost
position on a geared stepper. The whole stack ships as one ROS 2 Humble
container with CI on every change, so five people on five machines ran a
byte-identical environment.

### Noticing what changed inside the vessel between runs

Four chassis depth cameras and one arm camera back-project into point clouds,
merge in the body frame on a fixed-rate timer, and integrate into a persistent
voxel map. A map saved on one run is the reference for the next, and the diff is
published as a machine-readable summary. That diff is the product. Visual
odometry off the front camera is keyframe-anchored so matching noise does not
compound frame to frame, and YOLOv8 detection runs through ONNX on the same
streams. Because the cameras cannot stream inside the container, a synthetic
camera rig publishes the exact topic contract of the hardware, so the perception
tests run for anyone on the team without the robot.

### Wheel slip poisons the heading estimate

An Extended Kalman Filter fuses wheel odometry, visual odometry and inertial
data on the Jetson. Wheel odometry contributes translation only, because the
rear wheels slip under load and inject a phantom yaw rate the gyroscope never
sees. Visual odometry contributes translation only, because its rotation quality
collapses with feature count. The gyroscope owns yaw. Neither translation source
is fused as an absolute position, only as a velocity, since both are open-loop
dead reckoning and feeding their position in would inject unbounded drift as a
measurement.

### What ran on hardware

Holonomic drive under operator command with closed-loop wheel velocity and a
toroidal orbit proved. Visual odometry across four simultaneous camera streams.
Commanded arm motion through the reach envelope with object pick and stow, and
the 3 lb payload retained through the stow motion.
