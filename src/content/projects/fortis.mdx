FORTIS is a tethered, holonomic inspection and service robot for the interior of
General Atomics' DIII-D tokamak. I was the Software, Simulation and Vision Lead
and the overall technical lead on a five-person team. I owned the software
architecture, the simulation program, the perception and state estimation
stack, the containerized development environment, and Jetson Orin bring-up. My
teammates owned the mechanical design, the electrical distribution and the
fabrication.

<DataTable {...capabilityTiers} />

The constraint that shaped everything is access. The only way into the vessel is
the R0 port and a narrow entrance tunnel, which open onto a graphite-walled
interior with a low-friction floor and a step between the diverter and floor
planes. Nothing inside may be modified or contacted. Every external dimension on
the robot was fixed by a reactor built decades before anyone considered driving
a robot through it.

### Simulation

The budget did not allow physical prototypes, so simulation had to close the
design loop before anything was fabricated. I built the loop around NVIDIA Isaac
Sim: CAD geometry with real masses and materials went into a simulation model
with modeled wheels and reactor floor, out came torque, speed and stability
sweeps, and a sprint review gated on whether the criteria were met. Geometry did
not advance until numbers existed for it.

<Figure id="fortis-validation-loop" />

Two pieces of methodology carried the program.

The first is how to model an omni wheel in a physics engine that does not
support anisotropic friction. PhysX does not, so an omni wheel cannot be
expressed directly. I built each roller ring as a five-sphere chain instead, so
the geometry itself produces the free-rolling transverse axis. I characterized
the tradeoff rather than leaving it implicit: sphere rollers overestimate torque
relative to real free-spinning rollers, which means every motor margin the
simulation reported is conservative by construction.

The second is constraint-aware sampling. Rather than sampling joint space
blindly, I drew 5,000 poses uniformly across the four joints with a 3 lb payload
applied in every sample to hold the worst case, then filtered to the 2,014 poses
that are physically reachable inside the vessel. Each surviving pose was checked
for stability by projecting total center of gravity against the chassis support
polygon. That filtered set became the shared input for both the joint torque
analysis and the chassis stability analysis, so actuator sizing and tip-over
margin were computed against exactly the same population of poses rather than
two different ones.

The orbit sweep sized the drive motors. I ran one full orbit at each commanded
speed from 0.10 to 0.30 m/s, at a radius of 1.59 m, straddling the step, at
20.66 kg with the arm stowed. Worst-wheel 95th-percentile torque reaches 4.10 Nm
at 0.30 m/s against 4.98 Nm of continuous motor capability at 60 A.

the orbit runs in a fixed direction, so the load biases onto the front pair and
stays there.

### Software architecture

I built the stack as a containerized ROS 2 Humble environment bundling the
middleware, motion planning, the controller framework and a visualization
bridge, so every team member ran a byte-identical stack. That removed
environment drift as a category of problem across five people on different
machines. Continuous integration builds and tests the workspace on every change.

<DataTable {...architecture} />

The organizing property is that motion is state-gated. A table-driven mission
state machine holds nine states and fifteen events, with transitions and their
guards expressed as data rather than nested conditionals. It is pure logic with
no middleware or hardware dependencies, which is what makes it testable in
isolation. Every action component sits above it rather than beside it: the drive
component rejects any motion command issued outside a permitted mission phase
and emits an explicit stop on rejection, and the arm component was built against
the same contract, so inverse kinematics dropped in later without renegotiating
the interface.

<Figure id="fortis-software-layers" />

Two properties of that state machine matter more than the state count. Any state
can transition to the fault state through an unguarded wildcard, which is what
lets the drive-health monitor latch a fault from anywhere in the mission.
Leaving the fault state requires an explicit operator acknowledgement, so
recovery is never automatic: a drive that comes back healthy on its own does not
clear the fault.

<Figure id="fortis-mission-states" />

The drive data path runs from an operator teleoperation command, through an
X-drive kinematics component that maps commanded body motion to four wheel
velocity setpoints, out over CAN to four controllers running onboard velocity
control. The kinematics constants are pinned to the robot description by a
regression test, so the model and the code cannot drift apart silently.

### Perception and state estimation

Five depth cameras feed the stack: four on the chassis faces through a powered
USB 3 hub with a single uplink to the Jetson, and one on the arm. Each camera
back-projects its aligned depth into a point cloud, the clouds are transformed
into the body frame and merged on a fixed-rate timer, and the merged cloud
integrates into a persistent voxel map. A map saved on one run becomes the
reference for the next, and the difference between them is published as a
machine-readable summary. For an inspection robot whose whole job is noticing
what changed inside a vessel between runs, that diff is the product.

Visual odometry runs off the front camera and is keyframe-anchored: every frame
is matched against the current keyframe rather than the previous frame, so
per-frame matching noise does not compound between keyframe refreshes. Motion is
recovered by lifting matched features through depth and solving for pose with
outlier rejection.

State estimation fuses those sources in an Extended Kalman Filter running on the
Jetson. The interesting part is what is deliberately not fused. Wheel odometry
contributes translation only, never heading, because the rear wheels slip under
load and inject a phantom yaw rate the gyroscope never sees. Visual odometry
contributes translation only as well, because its rotation quality collapses
with feature count while the gyroscope stays uniform. The gyroscope owns yaw
outright. Each translation source fails a different way, one by slip and one by
tracking loss, so each is followed according to its own covariance and neither
is ever trusted for heading.

Because the cameras cannot stream inside the development container, I built a
synthetic camera rig that publishes the exact topic contract of the real
hardware. The whole perception chain is developed and tested against it, then
verified live on the Jetson.

### Design decisions

The drivetrain decision came out of a study rather than a preference. The team
started on skid-steer. I simulated the chassis performing the 90 degree point
turn it would need over the 4.5 in step across 60 geometrically valid
configurations. Three completed the turn. All three reached 85 to 116 degrees of
tilt and drifted 7 to 10 inches radially, which is not a usable margin next to
plasma-facing components. The reason is geometric: during a point turn every
wheel arc crosses the step circle, so the wheels must climb a wall nearly equal
to their own radius, and no wheelbase or track width avoids it.

<DataTable {...skidSteer} />

That redirected the architecture to an omnidirectional X-drive on omni wheels in
a chamfered square chassis, which also won on the primary functional criterion:
direct strafing for the toroidal orbit with no yaw required to translate. The
wheels never cross the step at all, because the robot straddles it instead of
turning over it. X-drive beat mecanum on mechanical grounds, since 90 degree
rollers are simpler than 45 degree ones and the 45 degree corner mounting falls
naturally out of the chamfered chassis.

<Figure id="fortis-internal-layout" />

Arm reach was set at 30 in rather than 50 in. At 50 in the arm needed oversized
actuators to carry its own weight plus payload, which broke the budget. At 30 in
it stayed inside the available stepper torque range while still meeting the
sponsor requirement. Links moved from aluminum to carbon fiber tubing: aluminum
with external wiring cascaded into larger and costlier motors, while carbon
fiber cut link mass and let the wiring route internally through hollow tubes.
Three links collapse to a 16.877 by 2.744 in stow profile.

Coordinated multi-axis stepping runs on a dedicated microcontroller rather than
the host, so trajectory timing does not depend on the operating system
scheduler. The host holds the analytic inverse kinematics and the motion action
interface; the microcontroller holds the step timing.

### Demonstrated

Holonomic chassis drive under operator command with closed-loop wheel velocity,
and toroidal orbit motion proved. Visual odometry across four simultaneous
camera streams with inertial data retrieval. Commanded arm motion through the
reach envelope with object pick and stow, and payload retained through the stow
motion.
