FORTIS is a tethered inspection robot for the interior of General Atomics'
DIII-D tokamak. I was the simulation and software lead on a five-person team
and the technical lead overall: the Isaac Sim program, the ROS 2 workspace and
its containers and CI, the mission state machine, drive control, the arm motion
path, perception, state estimation and the synthetic test rig. Teammates owned
the mechanicals, the power system and the arm firmware.

The constraints: a low-friction graphite floor nobody could test on, a vessel
nothing may touch, no budget for prototypes, and a robot that did not exist for
most of the term. Each section below is a problem that followed and what I
built for it. The full write-up linked above has the figures, tables and demo
clips for each.

### Free-body diagrams were answering the wrong question

The program started with free-body motor sizing around a tank-tread chassis.
The first physics test I ran in Isaac Sim showed a silicone tread could not hold
traction on the graphite slope: friction, not torque, was the binding
constraint. From then on nothing was procured until it had been simulated
against the reactor mesh, which I reduced from a 400 GB tile-resolved assembly
to a hand-authored collision surface after MuJoCo crashed on it.

### The physics engine cannot model an omni wheel

PhysX has no anisotropic friction, so a wheel that grips along its drive axis
and rolls freely across it cannot be declared as a material. Three iterations:
rings of spheres, then the real roller meshes as a 44 degree-of-freedom
articulation with convex hulls, then five-sphere roller colliders with
deliberate gaps after a single sphere sank through the hub and seven spheres
over-torqued. The model reads about 20 percent high on torque, so every motor
margin it reported is conservative by construction.

### The skid-steer chassis could not turn on the step

I wrote a parametric robot builder and swept 60 configurations through the
point turn a skid-steer robot needs over the step. Three completed it, all at 85
to 116 degrees of tilt, because every wheel arc crosses the step mid-turn. That
study moved the program to an X-drive on omni wheels.

### A joint space that is mostly invalid

Sizing the arm actuators meant sweeping a joint space that mostly puts the arm
through the chassis, floor or vessel. I built a three-stage pipeline: a headless
Monte Carlo of 5,000 poses over all four joints, an analytical post-filter for
collision, envelope and tip-over that flags rather than drops, and plots. Every
pose carries an analytic and a physics tip flag plus their disagreement, physics
runs at the lightest plausible chassis mass, and the filtered set feeds both the
torque and the stability analyses so they describe the same robot.

### Motion is state-gated

A table-driven mission state machine, pure Python with no ROS dependency, holds
nine states and fifteen events with guards over a context dictionary, so it was
exercised exhaustively before it ran on a robot. Drive and arm sit above it and
reject commands outside a permitted phase with explicit zeros, never silence.
Three independent stop mechanisms, a separate arming lock, and a health bridge
that folds the motor controllers' latched disarm reason into the error field so
a fault cannot clear itself. Fault is reachable from any state; leaving it needs
an operator acknowledgement.

### Drive control on a floor that slips

X-drive kinematics with uniform saturation so an over-limit command keeps its
direction, through ros2_control to a velocity controller on the motor bus, with
a mock hardware plugin so CI exercises the same controller config. A regression
test pins the kinematics constants to the URDF. A heading hold runs a PID on the
gyroscope-owned yaw estimate so it never chases wheel slip; an orbit generator
turns a held button into a constant body twist that keeps the cameras on the
center column. Off-robot UMBmark calibration and a velocity-loop autotuner
close the loop on the real drivetrain.

### A description built for MoveIt 2, and the arm behind one contract

The CAD tool's URDF export had closed loops, no collision geometry and no joint
limits, so I wrote the description by hand: 26 links and 25 joints of clean
primitives, limits on every arm joint, a mimic gripper, a tool center point for
grasp planning, and a ros2_control block behind an argument. The arm is a
MoveToPose action and two gripper services, gated on mission state. Behind the
action is an analytic solver, yaw decoupled and the rest a planar two-link
chain with elbow branches and deterministic pitch selection, closed to a
millimeter across 81 poses in test. MoveIt 2 ships in both containers with
cuMotion staged on the GPU image, and the analytic executor sits behind the same
action so a planner replaces it without callers changing.

### Cameras that could not stream where the code was written

A synthetic camera rig publishes the exact topic contract of the real driver for
up to four mounts over a raycast scene with analytic ground truth. The crux was
aliasing: point-sampled speckle rendered a different pattern from every pose and
visual odometry looked broken when the bug was the texture. The shader now
fades speckle octaves with per-pixel surface footprint, so a point renders the
same color from any pose.

### Noticing what changed inside the vessel

Per-camera clouds, body-frame fusion that skips a missing transform instead of
stalling, a sparse voxel grid with hit counts, and a cross-run diff published as
markers and a machine-readable summary. Detection behind one interface with a
blob backend for CI and YOLOv8 through ONNX for the robot, degrading to empty
detections rather than crashing. A click in the 3D panel becomes the mission
target and the event that advances the state machine.

### Visual odometry and wheel slip

Keyframe-anchored ORB with depth-lifted PnP RANSAC in a pure module; publishes
nothing on tracking loss so the filter coasts; covariance scales with inlier
count. On the public TUM RGB-D handheld sequence the core gives 0.052 m RMSE
trajectory error over an 8.01 m path with zero tracking losses. The EKF fuses
wheels and vision as velocity only and gives yaw to the gyro alone, because
slipping rear wheels inject a phantom yaw rate. Gyro bias is estimated only
while the drive is disarmed, the one trustworthy stillness signal.

### Testing a robot that did not exist yet

45 test files and 233 tests, run by CI on every push with per-package DDS domain
isolation. Integration tests import topic names from the production modules so a
rename fails loudly. The full-mission acceptance test drives the synthetic
camera, the perception chain, the state machine and the arm stack against a
firmware mock from idle through orbit, targeting, arm-at-view, pick, holding and
return-home back to idle, every wait bounded.

### What ran on hardware

On the sponsor's mockup: the full tunnel traversed, a toroidal strafe across the
step above target with zero tipping events, three of three pick-and-stow cycles
with the payload retained, and a 1.58 s E-stop.
