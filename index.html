A tethered inspection robot for the inside of a fusion reactor, designed and operated in simulation before it existed. FORTIS is a tethered inspection robot for the interior of General Atomics' DIII-D tokamak, November 2025 to June 2026. I was the simulation and software lead on a five-person team and the technical lead overall: the Isaac Sim program that designed the robot, simulated its missions and left the sponsor a reusable reactor environment; the ROS 2 workspace and its containers and CI; the mission state machine, drive control, the arm motion path and the arm firmware; perception, state estimation and the synthetic test rig. Teammates owned the mechanicals and the power system.

The constraints: a low-friction graphite floor nobody could test on, a vessel nothing may touch, no budget for prototypes, and a robot that did not exist for most of the term. Each section below is a problem that followed and what I built for it. The full write-up linked above has the figures, tables and demo clips for each.

Free-body diagrams were answering the wrong question

The program started with free-body motor sizing around a tank-tread chassis. The first physics test I ran in Isaac Sim showed a silicone tread could not hold traction on the graphite slope: friction, not torque, was the binding constraint. From then on the robot was designed in the simulator: nothing was procured until it had been driven against the reactor mesh, which I reduced from a 400 GB tile-resolved assembly to a hand-authored collision surface after MuJoCo crashed on it. That environment, with the wheel and arm models, is the asset the sponsor keeps for future in-vessel work.

The physics engine cannot model an omni wheel

PhysX has no anisotropic friction, so a wheel that grips along its drive axis and rolls freely across it cannot be declared as a material. Three iterations: rings of spheres, then the real roller meshes as a 44 degree-of-freedom articulation with convex hulls, then five-sphere roller colliders with deliberate gaps after a single sphere sank through the hub and seven spheres over-torqued. The model reads about 20 percent high on torque, so every motor margin it reported is conservative by construction.

The skid-steer chassis could not turn on the step

I wrote a parametric robot builder and swept 60 configurations through the point turn a skid-steer robot needs over the step. Three completed it, all at 85 to 116 degrees of tilt, because every wheel arc crosses the step mid-turn. That study moved the program to an X-drive on omni wheels.

A joint space that is mostly invalid

Sizing the arm actuators meant sweeping a joint space that mostly puts the arm through the chassis, floor or vessel. I built a three-stage pipeline: a headless Monte Carlo of 5,000 poses over all four joints, an analytical post-filter for collision, envelope and tip-over that flags rather than drops, and plots. Every pose carries an analytic and a physics tip flag plus their disagreement, physics runs at the lightest plausible chassis mass, and the filtered set feeds both the torque and the stability analyses so they describe the same robot.

Motion is state-gated

A table-driven mission state machine, pure Python with no ROS dependency, holds nine states and fifteen events with guards over a context dictionary, so it was exercised exhaustively before it ran on a robot. Drive and arm sit above it and reject commands outside a permitted phase with explicit zeros, never silence. Three independent stop mechanisms, a separate arming lock, and a health bridge that folds the motor controllers' latched disarm reason into the error field so a fault cannot clear itself. Fault is reachable from any state; leaving it needs an operator acknowledgement.

Drive control on a floor that slips

X-drive kinematics with uniform saturation so an over-limit command keeps its direction, through ros2_control to a velocity controller on the motor bus, with a mock hardware plugin so CI exercises the same controller config. A regression test pins the kinematics constants to the URDF. A heading hold runs a PID on the gyroscope-owned yaw estimate so it never chases wheel slip; an orbit generator turns a held button into a constant body twist that keeps the cameras on the center column. Off-robot UMBmark calibration and a velocity-loop autotuner close the loop on the real drivetrain.

The arm, from firmware to MoveToPose

On the Teensy, a control state machine over boot, disabled, enabled, moving and fault drives three closed-loop steppers and two servos with coordinated hardware-timed stepping, behind a CRC-checked binary serial protocol at 1 Mbaud with a 250 ms heartbeat watchdog that disables the motors on comms loss, and teach-and-persist homing checkpointed to EEPROM so the arm boots homed. The CAD tool's URDF export had closed loops, no collision geometry and no joint limits, so I wrote the description by hand: 26 links and 25 joints of clean primitives, limits on every arm joint, a mimic gripper, a tool center point for grasp planning, and a ros2_control block behind an argument. The arm is a MoveToPose action and two gripper services, gated on mission state. Behind the action is an analytic solver, yaw decoupled and the rest a planar two-link chain with elbow branches and deterministic pitch selection, closed to a millimeter across 81 poses in test. MoveIt 2 ships in both containers with cuMotion staged on the GPU image, and the analytic executor sits behind the same action so a planner replaces it without callers changing.

Cameras that could not stream where the code was written

A synthetic camera rig publishes the exact topic contract of the real driver for up to four mounts over a raycast scene with analytic ground truth. The crux was aliasing: point-sampled speckle rendered a different pattern from every pose and visual odometry looked broken when the bug was the texture. The shader now fades speckle octaves with per-pixel surface footprint, so a point renders the same color from any pose.

Noticing what changed inside the vessel

Per-camera clouds, body-frame fusion that skips a missing transform instead of stalling, a sparse voxel grid with hit counts, and a cross-run diff published as markers and a machine-readable summary. Detection behind one interface with a blob backend for CI and YOLOv8 through ONNX for the robot, degrading to empty detections rather than crashing. A click in the 3D panel becomes the mission target and the event that advances the state machine.

Visual odometry and wheel slip

Keyframe-anchored ORB with depth-lifted PnP RANSAC in a pure module; publishes nothing on tracking loss so the filter coasts; covariance scales with inlier count. On the public TUM RGB-D handheld sequence the core gives 0.052 m RMSE trajectory error over an 8.01 m path with zero tracking losses. The EKF fuses wheels and vision as velocity only and gives yaw to the gyro alone, because slipping rear wheels inject a phantom yaw rate. Gyro bias is estimated only while the drive is disarmed, the one trustworthy stillness signal.

Testing a robot that did not exist yet

45 test files and 233 tests, run by CI on every push with per-package DDS domain isolation. Integration tests import topic names from the production modules so a rename fails loudly. The full-mission acceptance test drives the synthetic camera, the perception chain, the state machine and the arm stack against a firmware mock from idle through orbit, targeting, arm-at-view, pick, holding and return-home back to idle, every wait bounded.

What ran on hardware

On the sponsor's mockup: the full tunnel traversed, a toroidal strafe across the step above target with zero tipping events, three of three pick-and-stow cycles with the payload retained, and a 1.58 s E-stop.

WALL-E block-sorting robot

UC Santa Cruz ECE 118/218 mechatronics

The interesting work was architectural: identical sub-machines handle every acquisition zone, so adding or reordering a zone is a change to the sequence rather than to the behavior.

Full write-up GitHub
Click to learn more Show less
RoleSole software developer
DatesSpring 2025
TeamTeam of three
StackPIC32, Embedded C, Hierarchical state machine, Input capture, IR and color sensing

An autonomous mobile robot that navigates a field, classifies colored blocks and sorts them into zones. I was the sole software developer on a three-person team; my teammates owned the mechanical design and fabrication.

Architecture

A top-level state machine sequences four phases: three zone-acquisition phases and a sort phase, terminating after the final zone. Each zone phase delegates to a sub-machine implementing one consistent acquisition strategy: advance under line-sensor supervision, halt at the calibrated sensing distance, classify the block, then either initiate a grab and report the color upward, or skip and continue. The sort phase dispatches on the reported color into one of two symmetric flows that turn toward the correct drop zone, release, and realign to the line.

Because the three zone sub-machines are the same machine with different parameters, reordering the field or adding a zone is an edit to the top-level sequence. The behavior inside a zone is written once.

Sensing is fully event-driven. Line detection and color classification each run as services that post discrete events into a shared queue, so the state machines never poll and never block on a sensor.

One deliberate simplicity decision is worth naming. Decoy blocks are handled statelessly: a decoy detection transitions the machine onward with no flag and no memory of having seen it. That removes an entire class of latch-up state at no cost to behavior, because nothing downstream ever needs to know a decoy was encountered.

The two diagrams above are the clearest artifact on this project. The first is the architecture as planned, built around an ultrasonic sweep to find blocks. The second is the architecture that shipped, driven by line events. The change between them came out of the bench work below.

Sensing

Rangefinder characterization drove a navigation redesign. The original concept swept an ultrasonic rangefinder to localize randomly scattered blocks. Bench characterization showed the physical limit clearly: the sensor returns accurate range only when the target lies near its acoustic axis, and during a pivoting sweep the wavefront strikes objects at oblique angles and scatters, collapsing detection outside a narrow frontal cone. Rather than filter around a measurement the sensor could not deliver, I rebuilt navigation around reflective infrared line following along taped paths. The rangefinder stayed in the system for what it does well, frontal proximity to a block during the approach, where the target is on-axis by construction, and came out of the localization role entirely.

Color classification. The color sensor was strongly sensitive to both standoff distance and ambient lighting. The fix was split across domains: chassis geometry was changed so a block is always presented at a fixed short standoff inside the calibrated range, and thresholds were then tuned on empirical data collected under consistent lighting. Classification requires a minimum separation between channels before it will commit, and a reading must repeat across consecutive samples before the event is posted. Constraining the mechanical problem is what made the software problem tractable.

Drivetrain asymmetry. The two drive motors were measurably unmatched, producing heading drift on straight runs and turn error. I characterized the drift through controlled runs and compensated it with per-direction drive-level offsets in the motion routines, which held alignment well enough for reliable line acquisition. The stated upgrade path is encoder feedback in place of hand-characterized compensation.

Event pacing. Direction changes introduce mechanical settling transients that corrupt both line and color readings if the next event is processed immediately. I introduced settling intervals between transitions and sensitive reads, then tuned them: too short and the machine decides prematurely, too long and it becomes sluggish.

Mechanical context

A 200 by 200 mm laser-cut tab-and-slot plywood chassis with 3D printing reserved for functional parts only, held to 14.244 in3 against a 30 in3 budget. My teammates owned this work; the one software-relevant consequence is the gripper standoff geometry that made color classification repeatable.

Leaf Me Alone

UC Santa Cruz ECE 167, sensing and signal processing

Water takes time to reach the sensor, so a controller that reads immediately after watering reads a lie and overwaters. The control strategy was designed around that delay rather than around a threshold.

Full write-up
Click to learn more Show less
RoleSensor characterization, signal chain and control
DatesWinter 2026
TeamTeam of three
StackSTM32F411RE, Embedded C, Kalman filter, ADC, Timer capture

Most hobby irrigation systems threshold a raw sensor value and switch a pump. That fails for a physical reason. Water takes time to percolate to the sensor, so a reading taken immediately after watering reflects the soil before the water arrived, and the controller keeps watering against a stale measurement. This system was designed around the delay with a pulse-and-soak strategy: deliver a short pulse, wait for percolation, re-read, and only then decide whether to pulse again.

Sensor selection as a characterization study

Both moisture-sensing candidates in the course kit were evaluated experimentally and ruled out on measured grounds before a replacement was chosen.

A capacitive touch sensor was proposed to infer moisture from oscillation period. Two measurable properties disqualified it. The first is timescale: soil moisture moves over hours to days through evaporation and uptake, and the sensor could not resolve gradual change between control updates even though it cleanly separated fully saturated from fully dry on the bench. The second is dynamic range: period variation between practical wet and dry conditions fell inside the sensor's own noise floor, so repeat trials at the same true moisture returned inconsistent readings.

A piezoelectric element was proposed to read soil compaction as a dryness proxy. Testing showed it is a charge generator responding to dynamic force rather than static load. Inserted and left stationary it produced a brief transient and settled to zero regardless of moisture or compaction.

Both were replaced with a dedicated capacitive soil moisture probe. That was chosen over the cheaper resistive style specifically because resistive probes pass DC through the soil and corrode by electrolysis.

Signal chain and calibration

The probe's analog output is read on a 12-bit converter and passed through a Kalman filter with process and measurement noise tuned empirically, which holds sample-to-sample variation under 1 percent volumetric water content in a stable environment.

Calibration endpoints were measured directly: 1210 counts submerged and 2523 counts in open air, a 1313-count dynamic range. Those endpoints map onto an approximate 10 to 50 percent volumetric water content scale for potting soil rather than a naive 0 to 100 percent, because 100 percent volumetric water content is physically impossible in soil. The endpoint values come from published soil-science reference ranges.

Reservoir level is measured by an ultrasonic rangefinder mounted above the reservoir, driven by a timer-based state machine with interrupt edge capture on the echo line. Time of flight converts to distance through a least-squares fit against measured known heights, is smoothed with a four-sample moving average, and maps to a 0 to 100 percent level through a two-point field calibration.

Control

The controller is a four-state machine updating every 500 ms: calibrate, idle, water and soak. Idle reads moisture and reservoir level, and enters watering only when the soil is below the trigger threshold and the reservoir is above its own floor. Watering runs a single 500 ms pump pulse. Soaking waits 10 seconds for percolation, re-reads, and either returns to idle on reaching target or issues another pulse, with a hard cap of 10 pulses.

Scope of the measurement

Volumetric water content here is approximate. True calibration requires gravimetric oven-dry analysis, which was not performed. The endpoints are specific to general potting soil and would move for a different medium, and moisture is sampled at a single point rather than across the root zone. Those bounds are stated because a moisture number without them is not a measurement.

BattleBoats

UC Santa Cruz ECE 121

Two microcontrollers playing a turn-based game over one wire cannot trust each other to flip a coin fairly, so turn order is settled by a commitment scheme rather than by asking politely.

GitHub
Click to learn more Show less
RoleSolo
Dates2025
StackPIC32, Embedded C, UART, Finite state machine, OLED

Two Uno32 boards play a turn-based game against each other over a crossed UART link. The game itself is the easy part; the parts worth writing up are the link and the trust problem.

The protocol. Messages are framed in an NMEA-like format with an XOR checksum over the payload, decoded a character at a time by a state machine rather than buffered and parsed whole. That matters on a microcontroller receiving one character per interrupt: the decoder holds a few bytes of state instead of a whole message buffer, and a corrupted frame is rejected at the checksum rather than producing a half-valid move. Five message types cover challenge, accept, reveal, shot and result.

The trust problem. Whoever moves first has an advantage, and neither board can watch the other generate a random number. Turn order is settled with a commitment scheme: each side commits to a hash of its secret, both commitments are exchanged, then both secrets are revealed and combined into the coin flip. Because each side committed before seeing the other's secret, neither can choose a secret that steers the outcome, and a revealed secret that does not match its commitment is detected as cheating rather than silently accepted.

Framing and pacing. The link runs at one character per timer tick against a 100 Hz timer, transmitting every tenth tick, so the protocol had to survive being spread across time rather than arriving as whole messages. The decoder is built for that: each character advances a state machine through frame start, payload, checksum and terminator, and an unexpected character at any state resets cleanly to hunting for the next frame start instead of corrupting the game. Host-side Python tools drive the same protocol from a PC, which is what made the link testable without a second board on the bench.

Game logic. A top-level state machine drives negotiation, move submission and win detection across seven states: start, challenging, accepting, attacking, defending, waiting to send, and the end screen. Board state tracks both fields on a 6 by 10 grid with four boat classes of three to six cells, per-boat life tracking, random placement with collision avoidance, and guess generation for the opposing side. Both fields render side by side on a 128 by 64 display, showing own boats and hits against unknown squares on the opponent's field, updated as results come back over the wire.

Ownership. Solo build: protocol, negotiation, game logic, field management and display.

Embedded FIR filter with persistent coefficients

UC Santa Cruz ECE 121

Filter coefficients that live in flash are fixed at compile time; putting them in external EEPROM behind a serial protocol makes the filter reconfigurable at runtime and across power cycles.

GitHub
Click to learn more Show less
RoleSolo
Dates2025
StackPIC32, Embedded C, I2C, SPI, ADC and DAC, FIR

A mixed-signal path on a PIC32: analog in, filtered, analog out, with the filter itself treated as data rather than as code.

The signal path. A four-channel scanning converter fills a circular buffer from its interrupt handler. A 32-tap FIR convolution with signed integer weights runs over that buffer, and the result drives an external converter over SPI, synchronized to the conversion rate so the output cadence is set by the sampler rather than by the main loop.

Why the coefficients are external. Compiled-in coefficients mean a rebuild and a reflash to change filter behavior. Here the coefficient banks live in an external EEPROM over I2C, with byte and page-level access and write-complete polling, so a bank survives power cycling and can be swapped without touching the firmware image. A checksummed packet protocol over the serial link updates them at runtime, with a packet queue so protocol handling does not stall the sampling path.

The protocol. Coefficient updates arrive over a checksummed packet protocol with framing, length, id, payload and an iterative checksum, decoded into a packet queue so protocol handling never stalls the sampling path. Endianness is converted explicitly at the boundary, which is the kind of detail that either gets designed in or gets discovered as a corrupted filter three weeks later.

Why the buffer is circular. The converter fills a circular buffer from its interrupt handler while the convolution reads behind it, so the filter always operates on the most recent 32 samples without either side blocking the other. The output converter is fed at the conversion rate, making the whole path deterministic from sample in to sample out.

Front-panel switches select the active input channel, switch between two filter banks, and choose between absolute-value and peak-to-peak display modes, so the effect of a coefficient change is visible immediately on the hardware.

Ownership. Solo build: sampling engine, filter, EEPROM driver, protocol and display.
