The problem

DIII-D is a fusion research tokamak at General Atomics. The sponsor wanted a tethered robot that could enter through a port, orbit the graphite floor, and carry an arm for camera inspection and small manipulation, with all compute on the robot and everything down one tether. The floor is low-friction and uncertain, nothing inside may be touched or modified, and there was no budget for physical prototypes. Every drivetrain and arm decision therefore had to be made in simulation against the real reactor geometry and be right the first time, and the software had to be testable without the robot, because the robot did not exist for most of the term.

Simulation

Free-body diagrams were answering the wrong question

The program started the way motor sizing usually does, with free-body analysis around a tank-tread chassis. The first physics test I ran in Isaac Sim invalidated it: a silicone tread on the 45 degree graphite slope could not hold traction, because silicone-on-graphite friction is too low to resist sliding at that grade. Friction, not torque, was the binding constraint, and the analysis had assumed the one thing it needed to prove. From then on nothing was procured until it had been simulated against the reactor mesh. Eight weeks of tread design were discarded, in simulation rather than after a purchase, and the slope was declared untraversable, which fixed the mission to the two lower floors.

A reactor model no physics engine could collide against

The sponsor's model was a tile-resolved SolidWorks assembly on the order of 400 GB. MuJoCo was the first simulator and crashed during contact resolution on the dense geometry even after reduction. I moved the program to Isaac Sim on PhysX 5 and built a reduction pipeline: a smoothed STEP export replaced per-tile geometry with continuous surfaces, the upper vessel half and outer shell that the robot never touches were removed, the slope became a parametric ramp for solver stability, and the open edges at the step were closed because they produced collision glitches. Collision was then authored by hand in the Isaac Sim GUI, and the result is committed as an artifact that is never regenerated.

Three engine constraints shaped every script after that. The Isaac Sim world singleton cannot be torn down and recreated in one process, so every parametric sweep runs one subprocess per test point and aggregates results outside. Triangle-mesh collision is static-only, so every dynamic body uses signed-distance-field, convex-hull or convex-decomposition collision instead. And the CAD could not be imported for physics, because gearbox internals form closed kinematic loops the articulation solver rejects, so every dynamic body was rebuilt from primitives with real masses.

The physics engine cannot model an omni wheel

Everything depended on simulating a holonomic drive on a slippery floor, and PhysX has no anisotropic friction. An omni wheel grips along its drive direction and rolls freely across it on passive rollers, and there is no way to declare a contact material that behaves differently along two axes. This took three iterations to get right.

The first model approximated each wheel as rings of spheres, 72 per wheel, which drove but carried more friction than a real roller. The second imported the real wheel meshes and built each roller as its own rigid body on a free revolute joint, a 44 degree-of-freedom articulation per robot, with a convex hull on every roller barrel so the anisotropy came from geometry rather than a material property. That was physically right and numerically wrong: under load a single-sphere roller collider sank through the wheel hub, and a seven-sphere version over-torqued. The canonical model became a five-sphere chain per roller, with deliberate gaps between the spheres, because a continuous ring of spheres collides isotropically and degenerates back into a cylinder, erasing the very freedom the model exists to capture.

I validated the five-sphere model against the single-sphere baseline with a full orbit sweep and kept the bias on the record: sphere chains resist more than free-spinning rollers, roughly 20 percent more torque on matched runs, so every motor margin the simulation reported is conservative by construction. A model biased the other way would have been useless for sizing motors.

The skid-steer chassis could not turn on the step

After treads, the team moved to skid-steer. To orbit from its landing orientation the robot has to point-turn while straddling a 4.5 inch step. I wrote a parametric robot builder and swept 60 geometrically valid configurations of wheel radius, width, wheelbase, track and torque. Three completed the turn, all at 85 to 116 degrees of tilt with 7 to 10 inches of radial drift, because every wheel arc crosses the step circle mid-turn and the wheels must climb a wall nearly their own radius. On the flat floor the differential wheel speed could not even generate the shear to rotate. That study, run with a friction coefficient more generous than the real floor, moved the program to an X-drive on omni wheels, which strafes the orbit with no yaw and never crosses the step.

Measuring torque honestly

The orbit sweep that sized the drive motors commands wheel velocities from a single inverse kinematics call after a settle and never corrects them, so the chassis drifts radially and tracking sits at 18 to 24 percent by design. An open-loop command yields an honest torque measurement; a closed-loop tracker fighting position error would inflate the reading. The same sweep exposed the radius drift as a controls problem: the yaw rate was computed for the configured radius rather than the measured one, so the robot spiralled inward. That diagnosis is what the orbit generator in the drive stack was later written around.

A joint space that is mostly invalid

Sizing the arm actuators meant sweeping its joint space, and most of that space puts the arm through the chassis, the floor or the vessel wall. A torque statistic over those poses describes a robot that cannot exist. I built a three-stage pipeline: a headless Monte Carlo sweep of 5,000 poses drawn uniformly over all four joints at a fixed seed, with the payload applied in every sample; an analytical post-filter for chassis, self and floor collision, the reactor envelope and tip-over; and a plotting stage. The base yaw joint is sampled rather than fixed, because stability depends on it, and the sampler's uniformity is checked before anything is trusted, since pass rates over a biased draw are not pass rates.

The filter never drops unstable poses. Every pose carries an analytic tip flag from projecting the center of gravity against the support polygon and a physics tip flag from the settle in simulation, plus a disagreement flag between them, so the two checks audit each other. The physics runs at the lightest plausible chassis mass and the margins are reported at the realistic one, an intentional asymmetry that makes every stability conclusion conservative. The filtered set is the shared input to both the joint torque and the chassis stability analyses, so actuator sizing and tip-over margin describe the same robot.

The ROS 2 stack

Infrastructure before nodes

Five people on five machines needed one software target, and the stack had to deploy to a Jetson. The effort began with infrastructure. Two containers share one ROS 2 Humble source tree: a CPU image that is the default for everyone and builds on x86 and ARM, and a GPU overlay on the Isaac ROS base that adds cuVSLAM, nvblox, cuMotion with its MoveIt 2 adapter and GPU image processing for the machines that can use them. Both carry MoveIt 2, ros2_control, robot_localization and a Foxglove bridge. A small shell wrapper turns bring-up, branch switching and exec-into-container into one-line commands, so flipping the Jetson to a branch is a single operation.

Lint runs on every commit through pre-commit and again in CI, and CI builds and tests the whole workspace on every pull request. Two isolation rules keep those tests honest: every package pins its own ROS domain id in a test fixture so parallel test processes cannot cross-talk over DDS, and CI runs packages sequentially as a second guard. Upstream hardware packages are vendored by a script from a pinned manifest rather than copied in.

Motion is state-gated

Inside a vessel that must not be touched, a drive or arm command arriving at the wrong moment is the worst failure available. The mission state machine is pure Python with no ROS or hardware dependency: nine states and fifteen events, with every transition and its guard expressed as a row in a table rather than nested conditionals. Guards read a context dictionary the caller supplies, so the same step with a different context is deterministic and the whole table was exercised exhaustively before it touched a robot. The machine can export itself as a diagram, a thin node bridges it to one topic per event and one per guard flag, and a terminal console drives it by hand during bring-up.

Every action component sits above that machine rather than beside it. The drive accepts velocity commands only in the orbit and return-home states, and anything else, including the window before any state has arrived, is rejected. A rejected command publishes explicit zeros on both the internal wheel topic and the controller's command topic, because a safety brake should never have to infer a stop from the absence of a message. Two more mechanisms stop the wheels independently of the operator's client: entering any non-driving state commands zeros at once, and a dead-man watchdog commands zeros if no velocity arrives within half a second, since teleop clients stop publishing on release without sending a zero and the velocity controller would otherwise hold its last setpoint. Arming is a separate lock: a UI node wraps the controller manager's switch service behind a plain Bool topic and reports the armed state from the service result, not the request.

Faults are edge-triggered and sticky. A status bridge folds the motor controllers' latched disarm reason into the active error field, because the firmware clears its transient error bits the moment a condition passes and a faulted axis would otherwise read clean. The bridge stops publishing entirely when any axis goes silent, so the health monitor's own watchdog trips rather than trusting a half-stale snapshot. The monitor starts with the drive reported unhealthy, flips true only after a clean snapshot, and emits the fault event on the true-to-false edge, which drives the mission into fault from any state through a wildcard transition. Leaving fault requires an explicit operator acknowledgement.

Drive control

The X-drive kinematics are a 4 by 3 matrix from body twist to wheel speeds with uniform saturation: a command that would exceed one wheel's limit is scaled as a whole so the commanded direction survives, and the pseudoinverse recovers the body twist from wheel feedback. The matrix stays ideal; the two hardware corrections, mirrored right-side motors and the camera-forward convention, live at the boundary in the drive node where they are documented on their constants. Wheel setpoints go through ros2_control to a joint-group velocity controller bound to the motor controllers over CAN, with a mock hardware plugin selectable from the URDF so CI and desk runs exercise the same controller configuration without a bus. Xacro tests expand every combination of those arguments and check the node ids, the joint lists and the URDF itself, since a swapped wheel on the bus would be nearly impossible to diagnose later.

Three constants tie the kinematics to the robot description, and a regression test parses the xacro and fails the moment either side moves. Beside it sit two calibration tools: an off-robot UMBmark calculator that turns measured straight-line and rotation errors into corrected effective wheel radius and lever arm, and a velocity-loop autotuner that steps each motor controller through a coarse-to-fine gain grid on the ground, under load, scoring rise time, overshoot, settling and oscillation, because gains tuned off the ground do not transfer to a loaded robot.

Two helpers sit on top of the command path. A heading hold arbitrates the operator's yaw command against a PID on the estimated yaw: while the operator is turning, their command passes through and the target tracks the live heading, and on release the controller holds the heading they stopped at, with stamp-driven timing and anti-windup. It works only because the estimate's yaw is gyroscope-owned, so the hold never chases wheel slip. An orbit generator turns a held button into a constant body twist of tangential strafe plus coupled yaw, with yaw rate equal to speed over radius, so the cameras keep facing the center; for a holonomic base that is a constant twist and needs no planner. Its own shorter dead-man makes release-to-stop crisp.

A robot description built for MoveIt 2 and ros2_control

The CAD tool's native URDF export was unusable for planning: closed kinematic loops with orphaned loop-closure links, no collision geometry, no joint limits, continuous joints where revolute was required, spurious prismatic constraints, and asymmetric gripper drive paths. I wrote the description by hand as xacro from the CAD treated as truth: 26 links and 25 joints as a clean tree of primitives with inertia macros, all dimensions in one constants file, revolute arm joints with limits, effort and velocity, a driven gripper finger with a mimic joint for the other, optical frames following the ROS camera convention so the depth driver plugs in without remapping, and a tool center point frame placed for grasp planning. The ros2_control block is included behind an argument, so the RViz-only workflow and the real-motor workflow share one file.

Arm firmware: deterministic timing and a fail-safe link

Step-and-direction control of a stepper needs pulse edges placed with microsecond determinism, and a general-purpose operating system can preempt a process for milliseconds, which on a step train is the difference between smooth motion and a stalled joint. So the arm's motion executor is firmware on a Teensy 4.1: a control state machine over boot, disabled, enabled, moving and fault, with a command-dispatch loop servicing the serial link between motion updates, coordinated multi-axis stepping for the three closed-loop stepper joints from hardware timers, and hardware PWM for the wrist and gripper servos. The stepping library first chosen would not compile against the current Teensy 4 core, so its Teensy 4 successor was vendored into the project and the firmware adapted to it.

The link is safety-relevant and conductor-limited through the arm, so it is a custom binary protocol rather than text or middleware over serial: fixed-layout frames at 1 Mbaud with a start byte, length, sequence counter, type, payload, CRC-16-CCITT over sequence, type and payload, and an end byte, with commands for enable, disable, joint targets, joint velocities, home and clear-faults, acknowledge and negative-acknowledge responses with error codes, and asynchronous boot and fault events. A 250 ms heartbeat watchdog disables the motors if host traffic stops. Homing is teach-and-persist: the operator jogs the arm to its home pose, one command declares it the zero and marks the joints homed, and the reference is checkpointed to EEPROM in ping-pong slots with per-slot CRC, so the arm boots already homed and a power cycle recovers a valid position rather than an arbitrary one. The cycloidal joints are near self-locking unpowered, which is what makes a saved zero trustworthy. Safety is layered: motion is gated on drivers enabled and joints homed, faults latch until explicitly cleared, an external emergency-stop input is honored, and the heartbeat is the comms-loss interlock.

The protocol was exercised first entirely in software against a pseudo-terminal mock of the firmware, then a host client drove the real board over USB, then the signal chain was verified at the level-shifter outputs before any driver was connected. One bring-up episode is worth recording: two joints held torque but would not step despite clean pulses on the oscilloscope. Tracing from the controller pin outward found two independent faults, one driver's control-signal selector left at the wrong logic level and one loose level-shifter jumper that scoped correctly on a high-impedance probe but carried no load. Verifying signal levels before connecting drivers is what kept those from becoming smoked hardware.

Arm motion and the MoveToPose contract

The arm interface was defined before its kinematics existed, because targeting and the operator interface needed a stable thing to call. A MoveToPose action carries a target pose in, progress out, and a success flag with a message back. The gripper is two Trigger services, because two endpoints map onto two operator buttons and two mission events. Both are gated on mission state with the same latched topic and the same throttled rejection pattern the drive uses, so reading one node tells you how the other works.

Behind that contract is an analytic solver for the four-joint arm. Base yaw decouples from the rest, and with the URDF's pitch axes the shoulder, elbow and wrist reduce to a planar two-link chain in reach and down-positive height with exactly two elbow branches. For a target with no requested tool pitch the solver scans candidate pitches nearest-level first and returns the first pitch that yields a limit-respecting solution, so results are deterministic. A test drives 81 in-limit joint configurations through forward kinematics and back and requires every one to close within a millimeter. The motion node solves every published target to drive the latched ik-ok flag that guards the transition from targeting to arm-at-view, reduces goal poses through TF into the arm base frame, streams joint waypoints at a configured joint speed with action feedback, and succeeds once the joint states settle inside tolerance or aborts on timeout. Goals are accepted only in the targeting, view, inspect and pick states; large repositions while carrying a payload are a mission decision, not an operator shortcut.

MoveIt 2 ships in both containers, with the cuMotion planner and its MoveIt adapter staged in the GPU image, and the description above was written so the setup assistant can generate a config from a clean chain. The executor that ran on the robot is the analytic path, and because it sits behind the same action, a MoveIt planner replaces it without any caller changing.

The serial bridge to the arm firmware is a background reader thread that reconnects forever, a heartbeat timer under 100 ms and a status poll, with status frames republished as joint states calibrated to radians and meters, a latched arm status message and diagnostics with decoded fault names. Firmware faults are forwarded as mission fault events. The frame codec is pure standard library and mirrors the firmware's framing exactly, so the bridge, the mock and the tests share one implementation, and the pseudo-terminal mock lets the bridge, the motion node and the full mission test run with no board attached.

Cameras that could not stream where the code was written

The depth cameras cannot stream inside the development container, so the perception stack would have been untestable anywhere but the robot. I built a synthetic camera rig that publishes the exact topic contract of the real driver, compressed RGB, aligned depth, camera info and IMU, for one to four mounts at the URDF poses, over a procedural scene of planes, boxes and spheres rendered by a vectorized raycaster, with closed-form orbit, line and hold trajectories that give exact ground truth beside every frame. Two scene variants differ by exactly one box added and one removed, both of known volume, so the map-diff tests assert exact numbers. Injectable gyro bias and noise exercise the debiasing path.

The crux was texture. Point-sampled speckle aliases once a pixel's footprint on a surface exceeds the noise cell, so the same wall rendered a different pattern from every pose, and that view-dependent shimmer corrupted feature matching. Visual odometry looked broken when the bug was the texture. The fix passes each hit's per-pixel footprint into the shader and fades every speckle octave out as the footprint grows, a small mip-map, with two octaves so surfaces stay trackable up close and across the room. Because checker and speckle are functions of world position only, a surface point renders the same color from any pose, which is exactly what cross-frame tracking needs.

Noticing what changed inside the vessel

A topic registry is the single source of truth for the perception chain. Each camera's aligned depth is back-projected into a colored cloud in its optical frame, dropping rather than queuing a pair that arrives while the previous one is still reprojecting. A fusion node buffers the newest cloud per camera, transforms all of them into the body frame on a fixed-rate timer, skips any camera whose transform is missing rather than stalling the rest, and voxel-downsamples the merge. A sparse voxel grid with per-voxel hit counts and first-seen color integrates the fused cloud in the odom frame; a map saved on one run becomes the reference for the next, and the diff is published as green-added and red-removed markers for the 3D panel and as a machine-readable summary of counts and volumes. For an inspection robot, that diff is the product.

Detection runs behind one interface with two backends: an HSV blob detector for CI and the synthetic scene, and YOLOv8 through ONNX with weights fetched by a pinned, checksum-verified download. Boxes are lifted to 3D through the depth median and into the body frame, and the nearest graspable object becomes the grasp candidate that guards the pick transition. With no weights on disk the node logs one warning, publishes empty detections at rate and stays alive, so launch files and CI stay green. A target selector turns an operator's click in the 3D panel into the mission target, validated against a range annulus and anchored in odom when the transform exists so the target does not drift while the robot moves, and fires the event that takes orbit into targeting. A health node grades every stream's rate onto diagnostics, and a missing publisher is a stale entry, never a crash.

Visual odometry, and what happens when it fails

Visual odometry off the front camera is keyframe-anchored ORB matching with a ratio test, depth-lifted PnP with RANSAC and a refinement step, all in a pure module with no ROS imports. Every frame is matched against the current keyframe rather than the previous frame, so matching noise does not compound and drift accrues per keyframe. The node pairs each RGB frame with the newest cached depth under an explicit staleness bound instead of a strict synchronizer, because on-device compression latency wobbles and a strict pairing dropped whole frames. On tracking loss it publishes nothing, so the filter coasts on wheels and gyro instead of ingesting garbage, and keeps publishing the inlier count as a cheap health signal; its covariance scales with the inverse of that count so the filter's trust follows feature support.

I validated the core two ways. Against the public TUM RGB-D handheld sequence with motion-capture ground truth, the integrated trajectory aligned rigidly to truth gives an absolute trajectory error of 0.052 m RMSE over an 8.01 m path, 0.7 percent, with zero tracking losses across all 792 frame pairs. Against the synthetic rig's exact orbit, the final error is bounded under 5 percent of path length as the acceptance test.

Wheel slip poisons the heading estimate

An Extended Kalman Filter fuses wheel odometry, visual odometry and the camera's inertial unit into one pose estimate, and the engineering is in what is excluded. Wheel odometry contributes translation only: the rear wheels slip under load and a slipping wheel injects a phantom yaw rate the gyroscope never sees, which makes wheel heading actively misleading. Visual odometry contributes translation only, because its rotation quality collapses with feature count while the gyro stays uniform. The gyro owns yaw. Neither source is fused as a pose, only as velocity, because both are open-loop dead reckoning and fusing their position would inject unbounded drift as though it were a measurement. The two filter configurations are held in lockstep by a test that asserts the visual variant is the base file plus one block.

The gyro carries a zero-rate bias, and a stationary detector based on measured velocity could never fire, because bias-driven creep keeps the wheels turning while the drift being cancelled is active. The bias is estimated only while the drive is disarmed, when the motors are idle and the chassis is still by definition, into a slow moving average, with a settle window after each disarm because the chassis rocks as the motors release; while armed the estimate freezes and keeps subtracting. Wheel odometry reads joint velocities by name rather than index, since the broadcaster does not guarantee order, integrates exactly through the yaw turned over each interval rather than a zero-order hold, which removes the inward drift a Euler step accumulates while turning, and runs on message stamps rather than wall clock so it survives delayed, bursty or replayed input. Its twist covariance is deliberately loose, because the wheel velocity reaches the filter stale after the controller, the CAN frame and the control loop.

Testing a robot that did not exist yet

Unit tests run real in-process nodes over DDS and assert on what comes out of the wire, not internal state. Integration tests live in their own package for process and domain isolation, since launched nodes linger in DDS state and cross-talk with per-package helpers. They import topic names, QoS profiles and event identifiers from the production modules rather than hard-coding strings, because catching divergence between producer and consumer is the whole point. The acceptance test for the full mission brings up the synthetic camera, the perception chain, the state machine and the arm stack against the firmware mock and drives the happy path end to end: idle to orbit on the start event, orbit to targeting on a click that clears the range annulus, targeting to arm-at-view when the motion node reports the inverse kinematics solved and completes the move, into pick on a grasp candidate from detection, into holding, into return-home and back to idle, with every wait bounded and the whole run inside a 150 second budget.

Operator interface

The operator drives from Foxglove Studio over the tether, on the same ROS 2 graph the robot runs, with four saved layouts: teleop with the per-wheel velocity plot and the arm, disarm, stop and orbit buttons; the four chassis cameras with one streaming per tab; the perception view with clouds, map diff and detections; and the arm panel. The click-to-target flow is the mission interface: a click in the 3D panel becomes the target pose, the state machine advances, and the arm moves.

What ran on hardware

On the sponsor's mockup of the port, tunnel and step: the chassis traversed the full tunnel, sustained a toroidal strafe across the step above the 0.1 m/s target with zero tipping and zero wheel-lift events, the arm reached 32.7 inches and completed three of three pick-and-stow cycles with the payload retained, and the operator E-stop halted all motion in 1.58 s. The mockup is plastic rather than graphite, so it confirms fit and end-to-end control; the low-friction margin remains established by the simulation. Every wheel torque figure on this page traces to a sweep whose raw CSV output and plotting scripts are in the repository.
