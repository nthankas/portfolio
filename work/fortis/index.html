FORTIS

A tokamak was never designed to be serviced by a robot, so the vessel fixed every dimension before design began. I designed the chassis and the arm, ran the simulation program that sized them, and built the software stack that runs them.

Context and constraints

DIII-D is a fusion research tokamak at General Atomics. Inspecting and servicing the vessel interior is currently manual work in a confined, delicate space. The sponsor asked for a tethered robot that could enter the vessel, orbit toroidally along it, and carry an arm for camera inspection and thermocouple placement on plasma-facing components.

The only way in is the R0 port and a narrow entrance tunnel. They open onto a graphite-walled interior with a low-friction floor and a step between the diverter and floor planes. Nothing inside may be modified or contacted, which rules out fixtures, rails, magnets and anything else that would make the problem easier. Every external dimension on the robot was therefore fixed by the reactor before the team drew anything.

The sponsor expressed the ask as four capability tiers. The fourth was descoped to future work by agreement early on, so the program targeted the first three.

Role

I was the technical lead for the five-person team and did the bulk of the engineering myself. The system design of the chassis and the arm, the drivetrain and arm sizing studies, the entire software stack, all of the simulation work, the perception and state estimation stack, the containerized development environment and Jetson Orin bring-up were mine. I also ran the technical direction of the team and the sprint reviews that gated design decisions.

The parts I did not do myself: the physical assembly and fabrication, the power distribution system, and a share of the CAD drafting, which ran under my direction. The drafting credits on the drawings on this page reflect that split. The hardware demonstrations below were run by the whole team.

Requirements decomposition

The capability tiers are sponsor language. These are the chassis requirements they decompose into, and they are what the simulation program was actually run against.

Chassis requirements derived from the vessel
Requirement Value
Cross-section through port and tunnel under 15.75 x 15.75 in
Step straddle, diverter to floor plane 4.5 in
Sustained toroidal strafe 0.10 m/s, under 2 in radial drift
Floor condition traction maintained on low-friction graphite
Reactor surfaces no modification permitted

One of these is subtler than it looks. The entry cross-section is not satisfied by the robot's driving posture: with wheels mounted, the chassis is wider than the tunnel. It is satisfied by entering on its side through the narrow face and being brought upright after passing through, which is a constraint on the entry sequence and on the underbelly geometry rather than on the chassis footprint.

Design decisions

Drivetrain: X-drive over skid-steer

The team started on skid-steer, which is the default for a four-wheeled ground robot and the cheapest thing to build. To orbit the vessel from its landing orientation, a skid-steer robot has to perform a 90 degree point turn while straddling the step. I simulated that turn across 60 geometrically valid configurations, sweeping wheel radius, wheel width, wheelbase, track width and motor torque.

Three configurations completed the turn. All three reached 85 to 116 degrees of tilt and drifted 7 to 10 inches radially. Next to plasma-facing components, a robot that nearly inverts itself and slides ten inches toward the wall is a failure regardless of whether the yaw target was met, so the honest reading of the study is that zero configurations passed.

The reason is geometric rather than a tuning problem. During a point turn every wheel traces an arc around the robot center, and the step is a circle those arcs must cross. The wheels therefore have to climb a wall nearly equal to their own radius mid-turn, and no combination of wheelbase and track width in the buildable range avoids the crossing. The three configurations that completed were the smallest wheel, narrowest tire and narrowest track in the sweep, which is the corner of the parameter space with the least margin left for real conditions. The study also assumed a friction coefficient more generous than the measured graphite floor.

That redirected the locomotion architecture to an omnidirectional X-drive on omni wheels in a chamfered chassis. It won on the primary functional criterion independently of the failure above: the mission is a toroidal orbit, and an X-drive strafes directly along it with no yaw required to translate, so the wheels never cross the step at all. The robot straddles the step for the entire orbit instead of turning over it.

X-drive beat mecanum, the other holonomic option, on mechanical grounds. Its 90 degree rollers are simpler and more durable than 45 degree mecanum rollers, 8 inch omni wheels were available off the shelf at the size needed, and 45 degree corner mounting falls naturally out of a chamfered chassis rather than being packaged in against it. Kinematically the two are equivalent for this application, so the tie broke on parts availability and roller robustness.

Arm reach: 30 inches over 50 inches

A 50 inch arm covers more of the vessel per chassis position, which is why it was the starting point. It lost on actuator budget. At 50 inches the arm needed oversized actuators simply to carry its own weight plus the 3 lb payload, and those actuators broke both the cost and the mass budget, which in turn raised the tipping problem for the chassis carrying them. At 30 inches the demand stayed inside the torque range of steppers the team could afford while still meeting the sponsor requirement, so reach was traded for a buildable actuator set.

Link material moved from aluminum to carbon fiber for the same reason. Aluminium links with external wiring cascaded into larger and costlier motors; carbon fiber tubing cut link mass and let the wiring route internally through the hollow sections, which removed the snag risk of external looms inside a vessel that must not be contacted. Three links collapse to a 16.877 by 2.744 inch stow profile.

Motion timing on a microcontroller, not the host

Coordinated multi-axis stepping runs on a dedicated microcontroller rather than on the compute module. The alternative, stepping from the host, was rejected because step timing would then depend on the operating system scheduler, and a late step pulse on a geared stepper is a lost position rather than a late one. The host owns the analytic inverse kinematics and the motion action interface, the microcontroller owns step timing, and the two meet at a serial contract with heartbeat and status reporting.

Simulation methodology

The budget did not allow physical prototypes. Simulation therefore had to close the design loop before anything was fabricated, and the loop was the deliverable as much as any individual number: CAD geometry with real masses and materials went into a simulation model with modeled wheels and reactor floor, out came torque, speed and stability sweeps, and a sprint review gated on whether the criteria were met. Geometry did not advance until numbers existed for it, and the bill of materials was locked only once the design stopped changing under the sweeps.

Modelling omni wheels without anisotropic friction

The physics engine has no anisotropic friction, so an omni wheel cannot be modeled directly: there is no way to declare a surface that grips along one axis and rolls freely along the other. I built each roller ring as a five-sphere chain instead, so the geometry itself produces the free-rolling transverse axis rather than a material property doing it.

The substitution is not free, and characterizing the error mattered more than hiding it. Sphere rollers in contact generate more friction than real free-spinning rollers, so the model overestimates the torque required to move. That means every motor margin the simulation reported is conservative by construction: the real robot demands less than the sweep says, never more. A model that erred the other way would have been useless for sizing motors.

Constraint-aware Monte Carlo for the arm

Sampling joint space blindly wastes almost all the samples, because most of the joint space puts the arm through the chassis, through the floor, or outside the vessel. I drew 5,000 poses uniformly across the four joints with the 3 lb payload applied in every single sample to hold the worst case, then filtered: 2,380 poses were rejected for chassis, self or floor collision, and a further 606 for leaving the reactor envelope, leaving 2,014 poses that are physically reachable inside the vessel.

Before trusting any of that I verified the sampler itself was uniform across each joint range, because pass rates computed over a biased draw are not pass rates.

Each surviving pose was then evaluated for stability by projecting the total center of gravity against the chassis support polygon. The design decision inside that check is the chassis mass used: I ran the physics at the lightest plausible chassis mass in the design range rather than the expected one, because a lighter chassis tips more easily under arm cantilever, so any pose that survives at the light mass is safe at the real one.

The important structural property is that this filtered set became the shared input for both the joint torque analysis and the chassis stability analysis. Actuator sizing and tip-over margin were computed against exactly the same population of poses, so the two results compose instead of describing two different robots.

The asymmetry is expected and bounded rather than a modeling artifact: the orbit runs in a fixed direction, so the load biases onto the front pair and stays there. It is also the reason the margin is quoted against the worst wheel rather than the mean.

Implementation

The stack is a containerized ROS 2 Humble environment bundling the middleware, motion planning, the controller framework and a visualization bridge, so every team member ran a byte-identical stack regardless of machine. That removed environment drift as a category of problem across five people, which on a one-term project is worth more than any individual feature. Continuous integration builds and tests the workspace on every change, and linting runs both locally on commit and in the same pipeline.

Motion is state-gated

A mission state machine holds nine states and fifteen events, with transitions and their guards expressed as a data table rather than nested conditionals. It is pure logic with no middleware or hardware dependencies, which is what makes it testable in isolation and what let the transition table be exercised exhaustively before any of it ran on a robot.

Every action component sits above it rather than beside it. The drive component rejects any motion command issued outside a permitted mission phase and emits an explicit stop signal on rejection rather than silently discarding the command, so a rejected command is observable. The arm component was scaffolded against the same contract before its kinematics existed, which is why inverse kinematics and the motion action dropped in later without renegotiating the interface.

Two properties matter more than the state count. Any state can transition to the fault state through an unguarded wildcard, which is what lets the drive health monitor latch a fault from anywhere in the mission. And leaving the fault state requires an explicit operator acknowledgement, so recovery is never automatic: a drive that comes back healthy on its own does not clear the fault, because the condition that caused it has not been inspected.

Drive path

Operator teleoperation publishes a body velocity command. An X-drive kinematics component maps commanded body motion to four wheel velocity setpoints, and those go out over a daisy-chained CAN bus to four controllers running onboard velocity control. The kinematics geometry constants are pinned to the robot description by a regression test that fails the moment either side moves, so the model the simulation used and the code the robot runs cannot drift apart quietly.

Perception

Five depth cameras feed the stack: four on the chassis faces through a powered USB 3 hub with a single uplink to the compute module, and one on the arm. Each camera back-projects its aligned depth into a point cloud in its own optical frame, the clouds are transformed into the body frame and merged on a fixed-rate timer, and the merged cloud integrates into a persistent voxel map.

A map saved on one run becomes the reference for the next, and the difference between them is published both as visual markers and as a machine-readable summary. For an inspection robot whose entire purpose is noticing what changed inside a vessel between runs, that difference is the product rather than a debugging aid.

Visual odometry runs off the front camera and is keyframe-anchored: every frame is matched against the current keyframe rather than against the previous frame, so per-frame matching noise does not compound between keyframe refreshes and drift accrues per keyframe instead of per frame. Motion is recovered by lifting matched features through depth and solving for pose with outlier rejection.

Because the depth cameras cannot stream inside the development container, I built a synthetic camera rig that publishes the exact topic contract of the real hardware, including aligned depth and inertial data for one to four mounts. The whole perception chain is developed and tested against it and then verified live on the compute module, which is what makes the perception tests runnable by anyone on the team without the robot.

State estimation

An Extended Kalman Filter fuses wheel odometry, visual odometry and inertial data into a single continuous estimate. The interesting engineering is in what is deliberately excluded.

Wheel odometry contributes translation only. Its yaw rate is not fused at all, because the rear wheels slip under load and a slipping wheel injects a phantom yaw rate that the gyroscope never sees, which makes the wheel-derived heading actively misleading rather than merely noisy. Visual odometry also contributes translation only, because its rotation quality collapses as feature count drops while the gyroscope stays uniform. The gyroscope owns yaw outright.

Neither translation source is fused as an absolute position either, only as a velocity, because both are open-loop dead reckoning with no absolute reference and feeding their absolute position in would inject unbounded drift as though it were a measurement. The two fail in different ways, one by slip and one by tracking loss, so each is followed according to its own stamped covariance and neither is ever trusted for heading.
