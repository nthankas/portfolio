Leaf Me Alone

Water takes time to percolate to the sensor, so an irrigation controller that reads immediately after watering reads a stale value and overwaters. This system was designed around that delay instead of around a threshold.

Context and constraints

A closed-loop autonomous irrigation system built for a sensing and signal processing course, running on a single microcontroller with the sensors available in the course kit as the starting point. The target was a system that keeps a potted plant inside a moisture band without supervision and without overwatering it.

The physical constraint that defines the problem is percolation delay. Water delivered at the surface takes time to reach the depth the probe sits at. A controller that thresholds a raw reading and runs the pump until the reading crosses will therefore keep pumping against a measurement that has not yet responded, and the plant is flooded by the time the sensor agrees. Any design that ignores the delay fails the same way regardless of how good the sensor is.

Role

A three-person team. I worked on the sensor characterization studies, the signal chain and calibration, and the control state machine.

Design decisions

Sensor selection, decided by characterization rather than by datasheet

Two moisture-sensing approaches were available in the kit and both were evaluated experimentally before either was accepted or rejected.

A capacitive touch sensor was proposed to infer moisture from oscillation period. It lost on two measurable grounds. The first is a timescale mismatch: soil moisture moves over hours to days through evaporation and uptake, and the sensor could not resolve gradual change between control updates, even though on the bench it cleanly separated fully saturated soil from fully dry soil. Separating the extremes is not the measurement the controller needs. The second is dynamic range: period variation between practical wet and dry conditions fell inside the sensor's own noise floor, so repeated trials at the same true moisture returned inconsistent readings.

A piezoelectric element was proposed to read soil compaction as a dryness proxy. Testing showed why the premise does not hold: the element is a charge generator that responds to dynamic force, not to static load. Inserted and left stationary it produced a brief transient as it was pushed in and then settled to zero, regardless of moisture or compaction.

Both were replaced with a dedicated capacitive soil moisture probe. That was chosen over the cheaper resistive style on a specific physical criterion: resistive probes pass DC through the soil and corrode by electrolysis, which makes them a consumable in exactly the deployment this system targets.

Pulse and soak rather than threshold and pump

The control strategy follows directly from the percolation constraint. Instead of running the pump until the reading crosses a target, the controller delivers a fixed short pulse, then waits for the water to reach the probe before it looks again. The measurement is only trusted after the system has given the physics time to happen. A hard cap on consecutive pulses bounds the worst case if the probe is ever isolated from the delivered water.

Implementation

Signal chain and calibration

The probe's analog output is read on a 12-bit converter and passed through a Kalman filter with process and measurement noise tuned empirically, holding sample-to-sample variation under 1 percent volumetric water content in a stable environment.

Calibration endpoints were measured directly rather than assumed: 1210 counts with the probe submerged and 2523 counts in open air, a 1313-count dynamic range. Those endpoints map onto an approximate 10 to 50 percent volumetric water content scale for potting soil rather than a naive 0 to 100 percent scale, because 100 percent volumetric water content is physically impossible in soil, and a controller calibrated against an impossible endpoint is calibrated against nothing. The 10 to 50 percent range comes from published soil-science reference values for the medium.

Reservoir sensing

An ultrasonic rangefinder mounted above the reservoir is driven by a timer-based state machine with interrupt edge capture on the echo line. Time of flight converts to distance through a least-squares fit against measured known heights rather than through the nominal speed of sound, is smoothed with a four-sample moving average, and maps to a 0 to 100 percent level through a two-point field calibration.

Control

A four-state machine updates every 500 ms: calibrate, idle, water and soak. Idle reads moisture and reservoir level and enters watering only when the soil is below the trigger threshold and the reservoir is above its own floor, so a low reservoir inhibits the pump rather than dry-running it. Watering runs a single 500 ms pump pulse. Soaking waits 10 seconds for percolation, re-reads, and either returns to idle on reaching target or issues another pulse, with a hard cap of 10 pulses per cycle.

That 9 of 10 trials converged on a single pulse is the result that says the pulse size and soak interval were matched to the soil, rather than the system simply iterating its way to target.

Scope of the measurement

Volumetric water content here is approximate. True calibration requires gravimetric oven-dry analysis, which was not performed, so the percentages are a repeatable index rather than an absolute soil physics measurement. The endpoints are specific to general potting soil and would move for a different medium. Moisture is sampled at a single point rather than across the root zone, so the controller regulates the soil around the probe and assumes the pot is representative. Stating those bounds is part of the result: a moisture number without them is not a measurement.
